# 69 - The FPS option and the in-between pictures (an addition to stage J)

Asked for by the owner on 2026-11-24, after the questions of how to show the game at 60 pictures a second without changing its speed, pitch or logic; reworked the same day after the owner played it ("nothing above native feels particularly smoother"). The Graphics Options (docs/68) get a third setting, **FPS**: how many pictures a second are shown. The **game itself never changes**: it takes one step every 1/59.92 s, driven by the sound device, exactly as in stage J; the FPS option only decides which moments of that game are shown. Source: `src/c/render/interp.[ch]` (the in-between pictures, portable C99, `ctest interp_check`), `src/c/render/render.[ch]` (the layers of a frame), `src/c/shell/menu.[ch]` (the option and the choice from the monitor, `ctest menu_check`), `src/c/platform/win32/win_main.c` (the timing and the window).

## 1. The choices, and what the game starts with

| Choice | Pictures a second |
|---|---|
| **Native** | **59.92**: the game's own pictures, one for each step of the game, as they have been shown since stage J; no in-between pictures |
| **75, 90, 120, 144, 165, 180** | that many |
| **Uncapped** | as many as there are: a new picture whenever the picture would differ from the one before (the moving things are placed to a pixel of the picture shown), without spinning when nothing moves |

**There is no "default" or "monitor" entry.** When the game starts it reads the monitor's refresh rate from Windows and selects the entry that is exactly it or the nearest number (`menu_fps_nearest`: 180 Hz gives 180, 59.94 or 60 give 60, 144 gives 144, 100 gives 90, 240 or 360 give 180; a tie goes to the higher; Native and Uncapped are never chosen for the player). The resolution is chosen the same way from the list of docs/68 (`menu_res_nearest`: the monitor's size, or the entry nearest to it; 2560 x 1440 here, exactly), and **the game always starts in borderless fullscreen**.

Everything from 60 up uses the in-between pictures below, made **by this program**: no driver feature (frame generation, "smooth motion"), no variable-refresh monitor, no GPU is needed or used for them.

**30 is different** (30 is no longer in the list, C-149; the still-frame mode stays in the code, `InterpOut.still`, and its test; changed after the owner's play-test, C-148). 30 pictures a second is fewer than the game makes (59.92), so there is nothing in between to invent; the first version still made pictures at 30 moments a second with the ghosts and Pac-Man gliding between their steps, and the owner saw "the whole game skipping and Pac-Man gliding across the screen": the playfield, the animation and the sound moved in jumps of two game frames and the sprites did not. Now **30 shows the game's own frames as they are, every second one** (`InterpOut.still`: the nearer frame, no object moved), a picture made **at the moment that frame is due** (no clock of its own, so it cannot drift against the game's, and no later than the frame): a game frame shown for exactly two game frames' time, the way a game made for 30 pictures a second looks. Measured (`--trace`, 420 pictures): every picture is two game frames after the one before (ids 2, 2, 2, ...), 33.3 ms apart, 99 % within 31.5 to 35.5 ms, with and without a shader. What this means for the animation: Pac-Man's mouth has four shapes (closed, half, open, half) and half of the game frames are left out, so it is seen as closed and open, or, when the picture frames line up with the half shapes, stays half open; which of the two depends on where the game was when the choice was made. That is what showing every second frame of this game does.

**The picture's game time (C-153): the cause of the pauses.** The pictures show the game at a game time `tau` that must stay behind the newest frame made (a picture needs the frame after the one it shows to see where the objects go). Until C-152 `tau` was a loop locked to the sound device's queue, which is read in steps of about 10 ms with a noise as large as the margin (0.4 to 1.0 frame). When the reading put `tau` ahead of the game, the picture was **pinned at the newest frame made** (`tau > newest` is cut to `newest`) and the loop took 0.25 s (a second after C-151, which slowed it to 3 s) to come back: for that time every picture was the newest frame as it arrived, the frames being made in bursts, with no glide and the animation in jerks: Pac-Man 'pausing' for a quarter of a second, whenever the noise did it, at every rate above native. Measured in fullscreen at 180 Hz: pinned for 0.75 s from 1.8 s into the run, again at 2.7 to 3.5 s; at 90, 3.2 to 4.7 s. **Now `tau` does not read the device at all**: it is a clock at exactly the game's rate placed so that `tau(made_j) <= j - 1` for every frame j of the last 2.5 s (each frame's making time is kept, `prod_ns`), changed only by 1 % (forward) or 10 % (back) of the time passed and left alone while it is less than 0.8 frame behind that limit (so that the pictures keep their place among the refreshes). The frames are made as the sound device needs them, so the picture stays with the sound: the picture is a mean of 1.5 to 1.8 frames behind the newest frame made, the sound about 2 to 3 frames, so the picture is about 0.8 frame (13 ms) ahead of the sound, as before. Measured (180, 90, 144, 120 Hz, fullscreen, 30 s): never pinned (0 of 5,300 pictures; the smallest margin 0.5 to 0.9 frame, the old loop 0.0), the clock's speed within 15 % of the game's in every 100 ms window. `tools/check_front_end.py` now fails when more than 1 % of the pictures are at the newest frame.

**The pauses at every rate above native (C-152).** After C-150 the owner saw Pac-Man pause for about a quarter of a second at seemingly equal intervals on every choice but native. The composed pictures were right (a hash of the picture around Pac-Man at every picture of the running program shows the stretches of unchanged picture where the game itself has them, at every rate), so the cause was in getting the picture to the screen: since C-150 the OpenGL window shows the picture whatever the shader choice, and every picture was sent to the graphics card whole (13 MB at 2304 x 1440), and every game frame the whole base picture was made again (3.3 million pixels). Measured in borderless fullscreen at 2560 x 1440 and 180 Hz: 2.96 ms to send a picture, 1.1 ms to make it, on top of the game, the sound and the speaker's filter; every third picture (the ones with a new game frame) did not fit in one refresh of 5.56 ms and took two: **126 pictures a second instead of 180, intervals of 5.5 and 11 ms in the ratio 2 to 1**, a hitch three times in a game frame (the windowed tests, a smaller picture, could not show it). Now only what changed is made and sent: when the nearer game frame changes, the bands of eight rows that differ (as wide as the change) and the places the objects were drawn at are made again (`rebuild_partial` in `render/interp.c`, checked pixel for pixel against a picture made from nothing for 600 pictures at two sizes, and that the rectangles given are enough to bring the last picture shown to this one, `ctest interp_check` `test_partial_equals_full`), and only those rectangles go to the graphics card (`crt_gl_dirty`). Measured again, same machine and mode: **180.0 pictures a second, none later than 1.6 refreshes (0 of 2,633)**; 75, 90, 120, 144 and 165: 75.0, 89.9, 120.0, 143.7 and 164.6 pictures a second, 0 or 1 late in about 1,000 to 2,000. `tools/check_front_end.py` now runs the game in fullscreen at the monitor's rate and fails when the pictures a second are not within 3 % of it or more than 2 % are late.

**How long each shape of an animation stays (C-151).** The in-between pictures show, at every moment, the game frame nearest in time, so every shape of every animation (the four shapes of Pac-Man's mouth: closed, half, open, half, one game frame each, 16.7 ms) appears, in order, none left out (measured on the running program for 75, 90, 120, 144, 165 and 180: no game frame without a picture, none out of order). **How long** a shape is on the screen is a whole number of pictures, and that is where the rates differ: 120 and 180 show every game frame for exactly 2 or 3 pictures (16.7 ms: as native); 90 alternates 1 and 2 pictures (11.1 and 22.2 ms), 75 shows one frame in four twice as long (13.3 and 26.7 ms), 144 and 165 alternate 2 and 3 pictures (13.9 / 20.8 ms and 18.2 / 12.1 ms). No picture can be on the screen for a fraction of a refresh, so for these four rates the mouth's shapes are not all equally long (at 90 the closed and the open shape are on the screen for 11 ms and the two half shapes for 22 ms); making them equal would take pictures mixed from two game frames, which the rule of this option (nothing mixed, nothing invented that the game does not draw) forbids. The game time of the pictures also goes by whole refreshes of the monitor when the rate fits it (180, 90 and 60 on 180 Hz), so that a picture that comes a fraction of a millisecond early or late no longer changes how many pictures a game frame lasts: at 180 the frames with 3 pictures went from 94.4 % to 97.9 % of all (2 or 4 pictures: 2.1 %, the beat between 59.92 and 180).

## 2. Why the first version did not feel smoother, and what is done now

**The game's motion is a staircase.** The original moves each ghost 2 pixels every second to sixth frame and Pac-Man 2 pixels every second or third frame (its speed pacing). In a bot game of 6,082 frames the gaps between steps were: ghosts 3 frames (717 times), 6 (300), 4 (48), 1 (183); Pac-Man 2 frames (1,259), 3 (580). That is 20 to 30 steps a second, whatever the rate the pictures are shown at.

**The first version** drew an object between *two consecutive frames* only: it glided during the one frame before a step and then held for the next one or two frames. Measured as the unevenness of an object's speed (spread / mean over steady stretches): 2.7 in the game's own pictures, 1.3 with that version. Twice as even, still stop-and-go: it did not feel smoother, and that is what the owner reported.

**Now** an object is drawn on the **straight way between its steps**, over the whole time between two steps, and arrives where the game put it at the moment the game did (`interp_follow`: where each object is, counted from the start of its run of frames, and whether it moved since the frame before; `interp_render(.., t, newest, ..)`: the game time `t` in frames). The game is 1 to 2 frames ahead of the picture, which is often the next step already. When the next step is not made yet, the object goes **on the way its last step went, for no more than one step**. Prototype over the same game (a scratch script, not kept: it takes the moves of each object from a replay, and evaluates the ways to draw an object; 5,300 frames, 5 objects, 180 samples a second): unevenness 2.68 in the game's own pictures, 1.30 for the first version, **0.34** with one frame of the game ahead, **0.26** with two; the prediction differs from the way the next step would give by 0.10 pixels on average, 0.56 at the 95th percentile and 2.0 at the 99th (an object that stops or turns goes up to one step the wrong way for a few frames). A longer look ahead would be smoother (0.14 with four frames) but costs delay between a key and the picture, which the owner's "feel" rule rules out.

## 3. How an in-between picture is made (`render/interp.c`)

- **The playfield** (the maze, the dots, the text, the status rows) is **the nearer game frame's**: not moved and not blended, so a dot disappears, a pellet blinks and the score changes at the moments the game did it.
- **The objects that move** are the four ghosts (the players of the Atari's graphics chip) and Pac-Man with the pop-up score (drawn with the missiles). Each is found in the frames (from the layers the renderer already had: `render_frame_layers`), how it moved from one frame to the next is **measured by matching its pixels** (a shift of up to 8 pixels), and the object of the nearer frame is drawn at the place the way between its steps gives for this moment.
- **An object that appears, vanishes, jumps more than 8 pixels (a ghost through the tunnel, back at the house) or does not match starts a new run and is not moved**: it is the nearer frame's.
- **The picture is made at the size it is shown at** (docs/68), so a sprite can stand a fraction of a game pixel off the place the game put it, and its pixels stay square blocks of the same size: nearest pixel, never smoothed. **No pixel is a mix of colours**: every pixel is a colour that GTIA's own rules give it (the priority between players and playfields, the colour registers of that line).
- The place of an object is a whole number of pixels **of the picture shown**, so two moments that put the objects on the same pixels make the same picture, and the program does not draw it again.

## 3b. No discrepancy with native: the rules, and the audit

The owner saw the score of an eaten ghost slide in "like a rubber band" in the in-between choices. Cause: the game draws Pac-Man's body and the pop-up score with the same graphics-chip objects (the missiles), and when a ghost is eaten Pac-Man vanishes and the score appears; the first versions matched Pac-Man to the score (half of the smaller shape's pixels overlapping was enough) and **glided** one into the other (6 pixels). The same happened to a ghost and the eyes it becomes (4 pixels). A bot game has 194 such changes in the 30 games of the regression set.

The rules now, so that nothing is shown that the native picture does not show:

- **Same object only** (`INTERP_SIMILAR`): two frames' shapes are the same object moved only when at least **45 %** of their joined pixels are on both at the best shift. Pac-Man chewing is 48 to 58 % between his three mouths (so he stays one object), a ghost and its eyes 14 to 33 %, Pac-Man and the score 14 to 37 %.
- **An object that is not the same in the next frame stays where the game has it**, at every moment up to that frame: no glide, no prediction towards it (the rubber band's second cause).
- **An object is never further from where the game has it than one step of its own** (the longest step it takes in the frames around).

**The audit** (`src/c/tests/interp_audit.c`, run by `tools/run_regression.py` on all 30 games; 214,917 frames, 5.2 million places an object is drawn at, the quarters of every frame with the game one and two frames ahead): **C1** an object that is not the same in the next frame is exactly on the game's place (0 of 7,080 failed); **C2** no object is further from the game's place than one step of its own (0 of 5,228,472); **C3** the way the match says an object moved agrees with the way its pixels' centre of mass moved, to 3 pixels (0 of 653,605); **C4** two frames called one object have shapes of a similar size, 50 % (0 of 653,605). The furthest an object is ever drawn from the game's own place: **3.00 pixels**. The audit is not a rubber stamp: with the old rule (`--similar 0`) the same game fails C2 72 times (the furthest 6.00 pixels), C3 and C4; the same audit over the 30 games and the three bot games of stage 87 together (237,300 frames, 6.0 million places, the furthest 3.00 pixels) was clean as well.

**What is still different from native, by design** (and measured): (1) an object is drawn up to one step off the game's place, towards where it is going, between two steps; (2) the picture is **11 milliseconds (0.7 frames) later on average** than the native picture of the same game step (0 to 27 ms), because an in-between picture must know where an object is going (see 4); (3) the native picture is shown when the step is made, about 38 ms before its sound plays; the in-between pictures are 0.6 frames ahead of the sound, so the picture leads the sound by about 10 ms.

## 4. Which moment is shown (timing)

- **The speed is the game's.** The game steps are made as before: whenever the sound device has less than about two frames of sound queued. 59.92 steps a second, 735.99 samples a step, no resampling: speed and pitch are the original's.
- **The picture follows the sound, a little ahead of it.** The time of the picture to show (in game steps) is the position of the sound device (the samples handed to it minus what it still holds) plus **0.6 frames** (`PICTURE_AHEAD`). The native picture is the newest game step, which is on average 1.28 frames ahead of the sound (0.4 to 2.2); the further behind that an in-between picture is, the later it shows what a key did. Measured at 180 pictures a second (`--trace`): the game is ahead of the picture by 0.96 frames on average with +0.3, **0.68 with +0.6 (the choice)**, 0.32 with +1.0, and 1.28 with no advance; the unevenness of a ghost's speed over those four settings is 0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 0.86 for ghost 0 and 1.46, 1.58, 1.65 for ghost 3 (a little smoother the further behind), but +1.0 has an uneven game time (the picture is often as new as the game allows, +-0.155 frames a picture against +-0.06). +0.6 is the nearest to native in time that keeps the game time even. That position is read in steps, so it goes through a slow filter (a model that runs at exactly 59.9227 steps a second and is pulled to the device's position with a time constant of 0.25 s): the picture moves smoothly, picture and sound are one (before, the picture was about two frames ahead of the sound), and the speed of the picture cannot differ from the game's. With `--no-audio` the system timer is the clock.
- **Ticks.** A rate that fits the monitor's refresh (every n-th refresh: 180 on a 180 Hz monitor, 60 or 90 on it, 60 on a 59.94 Hz one) is paced by the monitor itself (`DwmFlush`), so no picture is shown a refresh too long or too short; the others by a timer (the system timer resolution raised to 1 ms, the last millisecond waited for by yielding). Uncapped: no wait.
- **The menu and the help** (docs/68) pause as before; the picture behind them is the frame that was shown; when the game goes on the time model starts again from the sound's position.

## 6. Limits (OV-78)

- **The feel is the owner's to judge.** The numbers say the motion is smoother (a ghost's speed is 2 to 3 times as even), not that it feels it: the original's sprites move in 20 to 30 steps a second whatever is done, and the in-between pictures glide through them.
- **The way is a guess when the next step is not made yet** (the game is only 1 to 2 frames ahead): an object that stops or turns goes up to one step (2 pixels) the wrong way for a few frames. 5 % of the pictures differ from the exact way by more than half a pixel, 1 % by 2 pixels.
- **The picture is later than the native one by 11 ms on average** (0 to 27 ms): the in-between pictures cannot be as new as the newest game step, they have to know where an object is going; the playfield changes at the nearest of two frames (up to half a step early or late against a sprite that is gliding past it).
- **With a CRT shader on** (docs/70) the in-between pictures are made 8 times as wide and averaged back to 384 pixels, so an object moves in eighths of a game pixel with averaged edges.
- **The match of an object is by pixels**: a shape that changes at once with a move (Pac-Man's mouth, a ghost's feet) is matched by what the two shapes share; a few frames of the death or the ghost-eaten scenes may not be moved (never wrong, only not smoothed).
- **Limits of the measurement**: one machine, one monitor (180 Hz, 2560 x 1440); the pictures were counted and their places traced, not looked at on the screen or timed with a camera; a rate that does not fit the monitor's refresh (75, 144, 165 on 180 Hz) is timer-paced, so a repeated or skipped refresh is possible; the rates that fit rely on `DwmFlush`.
- **The start-up** is tested on this monitor only (180 Hz, 2560 x 1440: exact choices); the nearest-entry rule is tested by `menu_check` on other values but not on other monitors.
- **Windows minimises the test window now and then** (about 1 run in 4 of `tools/check_front_end.py`, never in a hand run of the program; the program was asked to say who does it (`logs/shell.log`, SC_MINIMIZE) and has not been told: it is not a minimise command to this window); the menu test restores the window before the next step and notes it (`NOTE`), and the check script repeats the menu test once when it was spoiled that way.
- The settings are not saved between runs.
